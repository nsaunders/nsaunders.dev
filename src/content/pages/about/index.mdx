I'm Nick Saunders, a Lead UI Engineer specializing in React, TypeScript, and
design systems. I started learning HTML in 1997. Today, I build web applications
and component libraries.

## Background

My professional career began with full-stack web development, from server-side
logic and SQL databases to the markup, styling, and behavior that define the
user experience. Early on, I worked primarily with C#, ASP.NET, and SQL Server.
Over time, my focus shifted toward JavaScript and frontend architecture,
eventually settling on React and TypeScript.

I began working on design systems in 2019. Since then, I've developed both
product UIs and the component libraries that power them, collaborating closely
with engineers, designers, and product managers to translate design intent into
working software.

Building and maintaining open-source libraries has deepened my experience with
API design, technical documentation, versioning, CI/CD, and release management.
You can find that work on the [Projects](/projects/) page.

I've also spent significant time studying functional programming with
PureScript. In addition to producing some interesting libraries, this has shaped
much of how I think about types and composition.

## How I approach it

Building applications informs how I design components. A component can look
elegant in isolation while lacking the flexibility required to solve real-world
use cases. My approach is to provide simple, robust, and accessible solutions
for common cases, while emphasizing composability for advanced scenarios.

Developer experience matters both for the consumers of a component library and
for the ones who maintain it. I want to understand how a component works by
reading its code, not by tracing unrelated stylesheets or figuring out which
props a parent silently forwards to it. That leads me toward explicit
interfaces, local reasoning, and strong type safety.

A useful abstraction hides the machinery while preserving the control its
consumers need. Sometimes that means a simple prop. Sometimes it means making
composition more explicit, even at the cost of a few extra lines of code. While
I value conciseness, I don't assume that a terse API is a simpler one. I
consider what work it saves, what it hides, and who has to deal with the
tradeoffs.

## Why I write

I write to share what I've learned building software—what has worked well, what
hasn't, and what I wish I'd understood sooner. I want readers to question
familiar approaches, including my own, rather than accept them because they're
popular or convenient. Simplicity is what I value most, but a convenient
shortcut doesn't always lead to the simplest solution. By working through
concrete examples and making the tradeoffs explicit, I hope to help other
developers think for themselves, avoid unnecessary complexity, and make
decisions they can explain and stand behind.
